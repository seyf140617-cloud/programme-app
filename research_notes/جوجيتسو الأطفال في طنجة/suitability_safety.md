# Kids' BJJ for a 10-year-old footballer: suitability, safety, choosing a club (Moroccan context)

Research date: 2026-11-07. Method note: most direct fetches failed because these hosts are blocked in the session: mmamag.ma, usadojo.com, journals.sagepub.com and bjj.space. Many findings below therefore rest on search-result snippets rather than full texts and are flagged as such. Firecrawl was not used.

## (a) Injury risk and benefits of BJJ/grappling for children vs striking sports

### Takeaway
The available data put youth BJJ competition injury rates low compared with adults and with judo. The best-known study found 4.1 injuries per 1,000 exposures in competitors aged 15 or under, against 10.8 for those 16 and over, but it counted only 5 youth injuries. The AAP (2016) says the main dangers in youth martial arts are head strikes, repeated head impacts on the floor and choking, and that catastrophic injuries are rare. Benefits evidence comes mostly from judo, where studies are small and mostly positive.

### Cited Findings
- **BJJ tournament study.** Overall incidence was 9.2 injuries per 1,000 exposures across 5,022 exposures. Competitors aged ≤15 had 4.1/1,000 and those ≥16 had 10.8/1,000. The youth estimate rests on only 5 injuries, so it is imprecise. Source is the snippet only; full text was not fetched. This is likely Scoggin et al., Orthop J Sports Med 2014. — [OJSM / SAGE, doi 10.1177/2325967114522184](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2325967114522184); see also [PMC4555620](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4555620)
- **Junior judo.** One study reported 39.8 injuries per 1,000 athlete-exposures for boys and 52.1 for girls, or 13.0 vs 16.7 per 100 athletes (417 boys, 270 girls). Being thrown was the most common mechanism, at 11.0/1,000 for boys. This comes from a secondary summary page. — [JudoInfo summary](https://judoinfo.com/?p=378)
- **Judo, all ages.** Competition injury rates in reviews range from 25.2 to 130.6 per 1,000 exposures. These figures are not youth-specific. — [Science for Sport](https://www.scienceforsport.com/injury-prevention-grappling-sports/)
- **Wrestling.** No youth figure was found. Adult freestyle competition rates are 13.1–42.0 per 1,000 athlete-exposures, and US collegiate wrestling about 19.6 per 1,000. — [Science for Sport](https://www.scienceforsport.com/?p=25977); [FICS 2025 combat injuries slides](https://fics.sport/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/SATURDAY-15-MARCH-_POLLARD-Combat-Injuries.pdf)
- **Comparison with striking.** A pediatric martial-arts summary gives 104.9 per 1,000 A-E for judo girls against 56.6 for taekwondo girls, from a single comparison. This shows that grappling is not automatically lower-risk than striking when measured in injuries per exposure. — [ActiveSafe martial arts PDF](https://activesafe.azurewebsites.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Martial-Arts_jj.pdf)
- **AAP clinical report.** "Youth Participation and Injury Risk in Martial Arts", *Pediatrics* 138(6), 28 Nov 2016, Council on Sports Medicine & Fitness (Demorest, Koutures et al.), doi 10.1542/peds.2016-3022. This is 2016 guidance and was still the main AAP statement found. — [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/exercise-and-fitness/aap-urges-clinician-counseling-of-safe-participation-in-martial-arts-717189.html); [eNewsPF press release](https://www.enewspf.com/science/health-and-fitness/new-american-academy-pediatrics-report-encourages-safer-participation-martial-arts/); [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/youth-participation-and-injury-risk-in-martial-arts/)
  - Reported rates are 41–133 injuries per 1,000 athletic exposures across martial arts, and very few injuries are catastrophic.
  - It classifies arts as striking, grappling or weapon-based, and says risk depends on what each art involves (kicking, sparring, grappling, takedowns).
  - It strongly discourages head strikes, repeated head thrusts to the floor and **choking** for youth, because of the risk of concussion, suffocation and spine injury. This was framed around MMA, but the choking concern is relevant to BJJ.
  - Contact and competition should wait until the child shows physical and emotional maturity and has mastered non-contact techniques.
  - Soft headgear and mouthguards have not been shown to prevent concussion.
- **Benefits review (judo, 2023).** Kowalczyk et al. reviewed 16 original studies of children aged 7–15. Typical training was 2–6 h/week for 9 months to 3 years. Most studies found positive health changes, and judo "may support WHO physical activity recommendations". — [PMC10453300, Children 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10453300/); see also [PMC8872033, principles of judo training for children](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8872033/)
- **Thin evidence base.** Gutiérrez-García et al. (2018) systematic review states that "scientific research on the effects of judo practice in children is scarce." — [CEJSH PDF](https://cejsh.icm.edu.pl/cejsh/element/bwmeta1.element.desklight-e07ed6e9-b9c0-433e-8574-8f050f5d2889/c/Effects_of_judo_participation_in_children_a_systematic_review.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a 10-year-old, the main safety advantage of kids' BJJ over striking arts is structural: kids' rules allow no strikes, and the rules ban dangerous submissions (see c). The AAP's main worry, head impact, is largely absent. The AAP's choking concern is handled by IBJJF kids' rules, which ban all submissions for ages 4–12 according to secondary sources, and by careful instruction in class.
- Takedowns and throws drive judo injuries. BJJ classes that start from the knees or teach controlled takedowns probably lower that risk. This is opinion and inference, not study data.
- None of the per-1,000 numbers are directly comparable, because exposure definitions differ and the studies are small and competition-focused. Training-session injury data for children in BJJ were not found.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed youth wrestling incidence per 1,000 exposures was retrieved.
- No training (non-competition) injury rates for kids' BJJ were found.
- The full text of the AAP report could not be fetched (usadojo.com is blocked), so its section on grappling specifics and benefits was not read.
- No BJJ-specific RCTs on psychosocial benefits or anti-bullying effects in children were found. Claims that "BJJ builds confidence or reduces bullying" are opinion or marketing unless sourced.

## (b) Training load for a 10-year-old combining football (2x/week) + a combat sport

### Takeaway
The widely used rule is that organised sport hours per week should not exceed the child's age, so no more than about 10 h/week at age 10. Football twice a week plus BJJ 1–2 times a week (roughly 4–6 h total) sits well within that. The guidance favours diversification over early specialisation, which supports adding a second sport at this age. The IOC 2015 consensus is the key multi-sport reference, but its exact hour figures were not verified.

### Cited Findings
- **IOC consensus statement.** Bergeron MF, Mountjoy M, Armstrong N, Chia M, Côté J, Emery CA et al. "International Olympic Committee consensus statement on youth athletic development." *Br J Sports Med* 2015;49:843–51 (2015 guidance). It identifies insufficient sleep, more overuse injuries, overtraining, burnout and eating disorders as concerns in youth talent development. — [IOC PDF via Sport Australia](https://www.sportaus.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0004/689647/IOC_consensus_statement_on_youth_athletic_development.pdf); [PMC8655480](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8655480)
- **Australian Institute of Sport position statement.** Hours of organised sport (training and competition) per week should not exceed the athlete's age, so a 10-year-old should do no more than 10 h/week across all sports. There is an absolute cap of 16 h/week regardless of the number of sports. — [AIS Sport Specialisation position statement](https://www.ais.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/716747/Sport-Specialisation-in-Young-Athletes-Position-Statement.pdf)
- **NATA-linked guidance.** Children should not play more hours per week than their age in years. — [Johns Hopkins Medicine](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/sports-injuries/youth-sport-specialization)
- **AOSSM consensus.** Early specialisation is defined as involving prepubertal children (about 7th grade, age ~12). — [NATA/JAT PDF](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/attr/54/10/article-p1040.pdf); [Leeds Beckett review](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/8010/1/RevisitingEarlySportSpecializationWhatsTheProblemAM-TILL.pdf)
- **Judo benefit studies.** These used 2–6 h/week of judo in children aged 7–15. — [PMC10453300](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10453300/)

### Inferences
- A practical plan is football 2x/week plus BJJ 1–2x/week of 45–60 min kids' classes, with at least 1–2 full rest days per week. Avoid BJJ the day after a hard football match if the boy is tired. This combines the hours-≤-age rule with general sport-medicine practice. It is a recommendation, not a numeric guideline from the IOC.
- Doing BJJ alongside football is diversification, not specialisation, which these consensus statements support at age 10.
- Common guidance also includes 1–3 months off a single sport per year, but this was not verified in this session's sources.

### Gaps
- The exact IOC 2015 text on hours and rest days was not fetched. Do not attribute "hours ≤ age" to the IOC; it comes from the AIS, NATA and Johns Hopkins.
- The AAP 2016 clinical report on sports specialisation (Brenner) and its "1–2 days off per week" advice was not retrieved in this session.

## (c) Kids' BJJ rules: restricted techniques and belt system

### Takeaway
The IBJJF kids' belt system (official 2022 document) runs grey (ages 4–15), yellow (7–15), orange (10–15) and green (13–15). Each colour has white-stripe, solid and black-stripe sub-levels, and at 16 the child moves to adult belts. Secondary sources say IBJJF bans all submissions for ages 4–12 and bans neck cranks, heel hooks, straight foot locks and jumping guard for kids. These secondary sources partly conflict with each other, and the official rulebook was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **IBJJF graduation system (official, 2022).** Grey group (grey/white, grey, grey/black) covers ages 4–15. Yellow group covers 7–15, orange 10–15 and green 13–15. At 16, grey, yellow and orange belts must become blue. Green may become blue or purple at the professor's discretion. — [IBJJF Graduação EN PDF (2022-11-09)](https://Www.Ibjjf.com/rails/active_storage/blobs/eyJfcmFpbHMiOnsibWVzc2FnZSI6IkJBaHBBcHNMIiwiZXhwIjpudWxsLCJwdXIiOiJibG9iX2lkIn19--d18b608b89ff1d36cdbbb52715af84a84f0ccc04/20221109_IBJJF_Graduacao_EN.pdf)
- **What this means at age 10.** He would normally start on grey/white. Orange becomes possible from age 10. — derived from the same IBJJF document
- **Stripes.** A 2011 IBJJF letter suggested one stripe per month for white and grey/white belts. This was a suggestion, not a binding rule, and it is older guidance. Stripe counts vary by school. — [Brazilian Black Belt 2011](https://brazilianblackbelt.com/2011/07/jiu-jitsu-kids-belt-system-from-2012-on-bjj); [Easton BJJ](https://eastonbjj.com/brazilian-jiu-jitsu/the-ibjjf-belt-system-for-kids/)
- **Banned techniques for kids (secondary sources).**
  - For ages 4–12, all submission techniques are illegal.
  - Neck cranks are not allowed for kids. One source says the ban covers all ages and belts.
  - Heel hooks are banned in gi at all levels.
  - Straight foot locks are not allowed for kids (legal for teens).
  - Jumping guard is not allowed for kids.
  
  Sources: [PBJJF Smoothcomp illegal moves table](https://pbjjf.smoothcomp.com/en/event/13892/page/33277/technical-fouls-illegal-moves); [Jiu-Jitsu News IBJJF legal/illegal guide](https://jiujitsu-news.com/?p=10384); [BJJ Fanatics](https://bjjfanatics.com/blogs/news/illegal-moves-in-bjj); [Grapplearts](https://www.grapplearts.com/17-ibjjf-bjj-competition-rules-remember/). These are secondary sources and conflict on details. Verify against the official IBJJF rulebook.

### Inferences
- If the rule that submissions are banned for ages 4–12 holds in a given event, a 10-year-old's matches would be decided on points (takedowns, sweeps, passes, mount) only. This greatly reduces the joint-lock and choke risks the AAP flags. Clubs may still teach chokes and armlocks in class under supervision, so parents should ask what is drilled at 10.
- Moroccan events that use IBJJF rules, such as Tanger Open 2026 (see e), would apply IBJJF kids' restrictions.

### Gaps
- The official IBJJF rulebook (current version) and the AJP (Abu Dhabi Jiu-Jitsu Pro / JJIF-UAEJJF) youth rules were not retrieved. The exact AJP age bands and banned-technique list for under-12s are unverified. AJP is known to use its own youth categories and restrictions, but no source was retrieved.

## (d) How to evaluate a kids' coach and club

### Takeaway
Safeguarding bodies (NSPCC/CPSU, UK) advise parents to check governing-body affiliation, coach training in safeguarding and first aid, a written safeguarding policy, a parental observation policy, and rules against one-to-one unsupervised contact. No source gave an authoritative coach-to-child ratio for martial arts.

### Cited Findings
- **NSPCC Child Protection in Sport Unit (Sept 2025), "Combat sports: what makes a good club?"** Parents should check the club's affiliation to a national governing body, and coaches should have up-to-date training including safeguarding and first aid. A martial-arts safeguarding code exists. — [NSPCC CPSU](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/news-and-blogs/2025-09-combat-sports-what-makes-a-good-club/); [CPSU parent advice](https://thecpsu.org.uk/news/2025-09-combat-sports-what-makes-a-good-club); [NSPCC safeguarding in martial arts](https://sport.nspcc.org.uk/resource-library/best-practice/safeguarding-in-martial-arts/)
- **Instructor vetting.** A school-owner guide recommends identity verification and criminal/sex-offender checks for all instructors, assistants and contractors, refreshed annually. It also recommends a written parent-observation policy. This is industry guidance from a US source. — [Gymdesk safe gym culture](https://gymdesk.com/blog/safe-gym-culture-martial-arts)
- **One-to-one contact.** A UK jiu-jitsu club policy warns that instructors alone with a child (before class, in changing rooms) are exposed to allegations. Good policy avoids this. — [Kingston Jiu Jitsu child protection policy](https://kingstonjiujitsu.com/child-protection-policy)
- **Example national-body policy.** BJJA GB publishes a safeguarding policy. — [BJJA GB safeguarding policy](https://bjjagb.com/download/34/policies/5806/bjja-gb-safeguarding-policy.pdf)
- **AAP on timing.** Children should master non-contact technique before contact or competition. — [HealthDay on AAP 2016](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/exercise-and-fitness/aap-urges-clinician-counseling-of-safe-participation-in-martial-arts-717189.html)

### Inferences — practical club-visit checklist (synthesised from the sources above plus AAP and IBJJF; items not tied to a source are marked as opinion)
1. **Affiliation.** Is the club affiliated with the Moroccan federation (FRMJJB) or a recognised team, and is the head coach's belt and lineage verifiable? (NSPCC principle)
2. **Coach credentials.** Look for a black belt or at least brown belt head coach with kids-specific experience, and first-aid and safeguarding training. Check whether any national coaching diploma exists in Morocco (see e, gap).
3. **Separate kids' classes by age and size.** For example, 7–10 and 11–14. A 10-year-old should not roll with adults or teenagers much heavier than him. (opinion and common practice)
4. **Class size and supervision.** Ask for the ratio. An assistant per roughly 10–12 kids is a common practitioner rule of thumb, but no authoritative source was found (opinion).
5. **Watch a full class.** Look for a warm-up, technique taught step by step, positional sparring before free rolling, and the coach stopping rolls quickly. Check whether submissions are restricted at age 10 in line with IBJJF kids' rules, and whether "tap early, release immediately" is taught explicitly.
6. **No unsupervised one-to-one contact.** Parents should be allowed to observe, under a written policy. Changing-room arrangements should be clear.
7. **Hygiene.** Mats should be cleaned daily, kids should wear clean gis and shower after class, and there should be a policy of no training with skin lesions (ringworm, impetigo). This is opinion and standard grappling practice; no source was retrieved in this session.
8. **Insurance and licence.** Is the child licensed and insured through the federation? Is a medical certificate on file?
9. **Competition pressure.** Competition should be optional, with no weight cutting for children. (AAP maturity principle plus opinion)
10. **Trial class.** Does the club offer a free trial, a clear fee structure and clear belt-promotion criteria?

### Gaps
- No authoritative coach-to-child ratio for kids' martial arts was found.
- There is no Moroccan equivalent of DBS or background checks in the sources. Moroccan safeguarding requirements for sports clubs are unknown.

## (e) Moroccan context

### Takeaway
BJJ in Morocco is governed by the Fédération Royale Marocaine de Jiu-Jitsu Brésilien et Sports Assimilés (FRMJJB), which has existed since at least 2019. In 2026 its position is contested. Moroccan MMA press reports that it is "marginalised" by the sports ministry, that a rival "league" calling itself a royal federation ran an unauthorised international grappling event, and that the federation took legal action. A "Tanger Open Tournament" under IBJJF rules was listed for 25 Jan 2026, organised by "LRTTH de Ju Jitsu". That body is likely the regional league for Tanger-Tétouan-Al Hoceima, but it may belong to Japanese ju-jitsu rather than BJJ.

### Cited Findings
- **2019 event.** The FRMJJB announced the "first BJJ tournament in Africa" (ADCC Open Africa) in Rabat for March 2019. This is older and shows the federation existed by 2019. — [Hespress FR](https://fr.hespress.com/52162-rabat-abrite-en-mars-prochain-le-premier-tournoi-de-jiu-jitsu-bresilien-en-afrique.html)
- **Rival league, March 2026.** An article dated 30 March 2026 reports that a rival league calling itself the Royal Federation held an international grappling championship without FRMJJB authorisation, despite federation warnings. The FRMJJB took legal action. Source is a search snippet only (site fetch blocked), and the date may be the site's display date. — [mmamag.ma](https://mmamag.ma/en/?p=5341)
- **Ministry marginalisation, February 2026.** A piece dated 13 Feb 2026 says the federation, led by **Jamal El-Soussi** according to the snippet, suffers "clear marginalisation" by the ministry in charge of sports. A related Arabic piece reports on "the party behind the campaign against BJJ in Morocco". Both are snippets only, from a single outlet. — [mmamag.ma ?p=5109](https://mmamag.ma/en/?p=5109); [mmamag.ma ?p=3834](https://mmamag.ma/en/?p=3834)
- **Growth.** The sport is reported to be growing fast in Morocco, with Grappling Industries announcing an event. The article is undated. — [EcoActu](https://ecoactu.ma/maroc-jiu-jitsu-bresilien-grappling/)
- **Tanger Open Tournament (TOT), 25 January 2026.** The organiser is listed as "LRTTH DE JU JITSU". It uses IBJJF rules with gi and no-gi divisions. The listing does not confirm kids' divisions. — [bjj.space event listing](https://bjj.space/events/tanger-open-tournament-tot) (snippet only; fetch blocked)
- **Medical certificate for minors.** No BJJ-specific rule was found. As an analogy, the Moroccan swimming federation (FRMN) requires an original medical certificate of fitness, a birth extract and a notarised parental authorisation before licensing anyone aged 17 or under. — [FRMN Règlements Généraux](https://frmnatation.com/sites/default/files/R%C3%A8glements%20G%C3%A9n%C3%A9raux%20d%C3%A9finitifs_0.pdf)
- **Medical record booklet.** The ministry publishes a "livret médical du sportif". — [MJCC livret médical PDF](https://mjcc.gov.ma/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/livret_medical_du_sportif_16_page.pdf)
- **Legal framework.** Law 30-09 on physical education and sport governs sports associations and federations. Its implementing decree is Décret n° 2-10-628 of 4 Nov 2011. Commentators in 2023 called for its reform. — [Le Matin](https://lematin.ma/journal/2014/droit-du-sport_la-loi-30-09-epluchee-a-meknes-/202078/amp); [Medias24 2023](https://medias24.com/2023/04/07/reforme-du-droit-du-sport-au-maroc-propositions-pour-une-mise-a-jour-de-la-loi-30-09/); [CNOM statutes](https://cnom.org.ma/sites/default/files/Statuts_CNOM_240627.pdf)
- **Moroccan grappling is fragmented across federations.** Sambo and tai-jitsu have their own royal federation (president Dalil Skalli, re-elected), which ran national youth sambo championships in Casablanca in 2026. Judo and assimilated martial arts sit under a separate federation. — [sambo.sport](https://www.sambo.sport/en/news/dalil-skalli-pereizbran-na-post-prezidenta-korolevskoy-marokkanskoy-federatsii-sambo-i-tay-dzhitsu/); [sambo.sport 2026 youth](https://www.sambo.sport/en/news/molodezhnoe-pervenstvo-marokko-po-sambo-proshlo-v-kasablanke-2026/); [SNRT judo](https://snrtnews.com/fr/article/judo-et-arts-martiaux-assimiles-le-maroc-boycotte-deux-manifestations-sportives-abritees)

### Inferences
- Parents in Tangier should ask a club which federation it is licensed under: FRMJJB, the rival grappling league, or the judo or ju-jitsu federation. Given the 2026 dispute, a licence from an unrecognised body may not carry valid insurance or allow entry to official competitions. This is an inference from the reported dispute.
- Expect Moroccan clubs to require, for a licence, a medical certificate of fitness for the specific sport, a birth extract and parental authorisation. This is extrapolated from FRMN practice and is not confirmed for BJJ.
- "LRTTH" most likely stands for Ligue Régionale Tanger-Tétouan-Al Hoceima. "Ju Jitsu" may refer to the Japanese ju-jitsu league under the judo federation rather than the FRMJJB. This needs confirmation.

### Gaps

- No Moroccan BJJ coach certification scheme (federation diploma or ministry "brevet d'État") was found.
- The existence and contacts of a FRMJJB regional league for Tanger-Tétouan-Al Hoceima were not confirmed. Neither was whether TOT 2026 had kids' divisions.
