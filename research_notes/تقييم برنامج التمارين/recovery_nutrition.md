# Recovery Modalities and Nutrition for Muscle Gain: Applied to the User's 4-Day Upper/Lower Plan

Context check (from program.txt): lifting on Mon (Upper A), Wed (Lower A), Fri (Upper B), Sat (Lower B). Tue/Thu are "active recovery": light swim 20–30 min OR treadmill 20–25 min at 5.2 km/h 0% incline, sauna 10–15 min, cold tub 1–3 min. Sunday is full rest (optional forearm work). Note: the Tue/Thu day headers in program.txt say "سباحة + سونا + جاكوزي" (swim + sauna + jacuzzi), but the items list "Cold Tub 1–3 min". The writer should flag this naming inconsistency (a jacuzzi is a hot tub, not a cold tub). User: ~73 kg, recreational, goal hypertrophy. Not medical advice.

## Q1. Cold-water immersion (CWI) and hypertrophy: does it blunt gains, and do timing (rest days) and short duration (1–3 min) matter?

### Takeaway
CWI done right after lifting fairly consistently blunts hypertrophy. The best summary is Piñero 2024, which found a small effect with roughly 96% probability that it is real. Every trial that shows this used about 10–15 min at about 10 °C within minutes of the lifting session. The user's routine is different on both counts: 1–3 min on non-lifting days, about 24 h after a session. No study has tested that exact pattern, so the risk is plausibly much smaller, but it has not been shown to be zero.

### Cited Findings
- **Roberts et al. 2015, J Physiol 593:4285–4301 (RCT).** 21 physically active men did strength training 2 days/wk for 12 weeks, followed by either 10 min CWI or active recovery (low-intensity cycling) after each session. The active-recovery group gained more muscle mass and strength. Type II fibre CSA rose 17% and myonuclei per fibre rose 26% with active recovery, but neither changed with CWI. In a second acute study (n=9), p70S6K phosphorylation and satellite-cell (Pax7+, NCAM+) responses at 24–48 h were smaller after CWI. — [PubMed 26174323](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26174323/)
- **Fyfe et al. 2019, J Appl Physiol 127(5) (RCT).** 16 men trained 3 days/wk for 7 weeks, with CWI at 10 °C for 15 min after each session versus a 23 °C passive control. CWI reduced the gain in type II fibre CSA (−1,959 µm²; ES −1.37). Gains in DXA lean mass (+2.6 kg pooled, no group × time interaction) and 1-RM leg press were similar in both groups. rpS6 (mTORC1) signalling was blunted at +1 h and at +48 h, and FOXO1, a marker of protein breakdown, rose 1.3-fold. Authors' conclusion: "Post-exercise CWI should therefore be avoided if muscle hypertrophy is desired." — [J Appl Physiol](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/japplphysiol.00127.2019)
- **Piñero et al. 2024, Eur J Sport Sci 24 (Bayesian systematic review and meta-analysis, 8 studies).** Hypertrophy with RT alone: SMD 0.36 [95% CrI 0.10–0.61]. With CWI + RT: SMD 0.14 [−0.08–0.36]. Head-to-head comparison: cSMD −0.22 [−0.47 to 0.04], with p(<0)=0.957 and p(<−0.1)=0.834. Training status did not moderate the effect (−0.10 [−0.65 to 0.43]), and neither did shorter versus longer interventions. The authors rated the included studies as generally of fair-to-poor quality. Their conclusion: CWI "immediately following bouts of RT may attenuate hypertrophic changes." — [PMC11235606](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235606/); [RGU repository](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/output/2173897/throwing-cold-water-on-muscle-growth-a-systematic-review-with-meta-analysis-of-the-effects-of-post-exercise-cold-water-immersion-on-resistance-training-induced-hypertrophy)
- The Piñero preprint describes the typical CWI protocol as water below 15 °C for 10–20 min after exercise. It says CWI-related changes in signalling and satellite cells "persisted for up to 48 hours after application." — [ResearchGate preprint](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371449703_Throwing_cold_water_on_muscle_growth_A_systematic_review_with_meta-analysis_of_the_effects_of_post-exercise_cold_water_immersion_on_resistance_training-_induced_hypertrophy)
- A secondary source (FitChef, not peer-reviewed) says all 8 studies in Piñero 2024 applied CWI within 20 min of training and that none tested intermittent use or non-training-day use. This is consistent with the meta-analysis title ("postexercise"), but I did not verify it table by table. — [FitChef summary](https://fitchef.com/studies/cold-water-immersion-hypertrophy/)
- In the Fyfe and Roberts trials, the blunted fibre growth came with unchanged or less-affected strength and DXA lean mass (Fyfe). This suggests the cost shows up at fibre level and is small at whole-body level. — [Fyfe 2019](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/japplphysiol.00127.2019)

### Inferences
- **Timing in this plan.** The Tue cold tub comes about 24 h after Mon Upper A, and the Thu cold tub about 24 h after Wed Lower A. Neither follows a lifting session within minutes, which is the condition every trial tested. In Fyfe, signalling was still blunted at +48 h, but that +48 h result came from cold applied immediately after the session. It does not show that cold applied at +24 h interferes. The mechanistic window may still overlap, because MPS and satellite-cell activity stay elevated for about 24–48 h after a session. So rest-day cold carries an unknown but probably small risk.
- **Dose.** 1–3 min is roughly 5–15 times shorter than the 10–15 min protocols tested. Tub temperature is unknown. The muscle-cooling dose is likely much smaller.
- **Practical adjustment (conservative).** If maximum hypertrophy is the priority, there are three options. (a) Keep the cold tub only on Tue/Thu and never right after Mon/Wed/Fri/Sat lifting. That is already the case and should be stated explicitly in the app. (b) Optionally move the cold exposure to Sunday or to the morning of rest days, the furthest point from the previous session. (c) Drop it altogether if its only purpose is "recovery", because the hypertrophy-focused evidence shows no added benefit for gains. If the user enjoys it for alertness or wellbeing, 1–3 min on rest days is a reasonable trade-off.
- Lower-body work (Wed/Sat) is the main concern: legs are what get immersed, and every trial measured vastus lateralis or the legs. Thu cold follows Wed Lower A by about 24 h. The user could reverse the Thu order (sauna last) or skip cold on Thu.

### Gaps
- No RCT found that tests CWI on non-training days, at about 24 h after a session, or for 1–3 min.
- No data on how a hot-then-cold sequence (sauna followed by cold) affects hypertrophy.
- The cold tub's water temperature is unknown.

## Q2. Sauna and heat: hypertrophy, recovery, interference, safety

### Takeaway
The evidence on heat is mixed and low quality. Small or uncontrolled studies suggest heat can add a little muscle, and mechanistic work shows heat-shock and mTOR signalling. Controlled trials in trained people mostly find no extra hypertrophy from post-exercise heat. No study shows that heat interferes with gains. Sauna is therefore a neutral-to-possibly-helpful wellbeing tool here, not a hypertrophy tool.

### Cited Findings
- Kakigi et al. 2011, J Physiol Sci 61:131–140: heat stress enhanced mTOR signalling after resistance exercise in human muscle (mechanistic study, acute). — cited in [Podstawski et al. 2021, IJERPH, PMC8122786](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8122786/)
- Stadnyk et al. 2018, Temperature 5:175–183: "No clear benefit of muscle heating on hypertrophy and strength with resistance training." — cited in [PMC8122786](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8122786/)
- Hafen et al. 2019, J Appl Physiol 127:47–57: daily heat treatment maintained mitochondrial function and reduced atrophy during immobilisation. This is a disuse model, not training. — cited in [PMC8122786](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8122786/)
- Frontiers in Sports and Active Living 2025: 6 weeks of training produced lower-body hypertrophy, and post-exercise infrared sauna "did not contribute any additional changes in hypertrophy." The authors note that muscle temperatures of about 38–40 °C may be needed for heat-driven hypertrophy, and that sauna exposure probably did not reach that. — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1462901/full)
- Sports Medicine – Open 2025, systematic review of post-exercise heat exposure: it notes some evidence that long-term heating can increase hypertrophy and force in sedentary people, but "these results may not be generalisable to trained populations." — [Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-025-00910-0)
- MDPI Applied Sciences 2025 (4 weeks of 100 °C sauna plus RT, blinded randomised study): reports larger muscle mass gains in the heat group. It is small and short, so treat it as preliminary. — [MDPI 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/19/10762)
- Pryor et al. 2024, J Strength Cond Res 38:1350–1357: a review of the mechanisms by which RT in the heat might add hypertrophy. It is a narrative review, not outcome data. — cited in [MDPI 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/19/10762)
- Fyfe 2019 found that CWI reduced training-induced HSP27 and HSP72. Heat raises these proteins, which is part of why heat and cold are thought to act in opposite directions. — [Fyfe 2019](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/japplphysiol.00127.2019)

### Inferences
- 10–15 min of sauna on rest days is unlikely to hurt hypertrophy. Any benefit is speculative.
- **Safety (general physiology, not sourced in this session; the writer should present it as standard precautions).** Drink fluids before and after. Avoid alcohol. Get out if dizzy. Going straight from sauna to cold water causes a sharp jump in blood pressure and heart rate, so anyone with cardiovascular issues should get medical clearance. This is not medical advice.
- A sauna session followed by a cold dip ends on cooling. Doing cold first and sauna last ends warm, which may matter if the user wants to minimise cold exposure.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a peer-reviewed source on the cardiovascular safety of hot-cold contrast in this session. The writer should keep safety notes generic.
- No trial tests rest-day sauna in trained lifters.

## Q3. Concurrent training: does light swimming or walking on off days interfere with hypertrophy?

### Takeaway
No. The biggest updated meta-analysis (Schumann 2022, 43 studies) found no compromise of whole-muscle hypertrophy (SMD about −0.01) or maximal strength. Any interference shows up at fibre level, mostly with running, and involves loads far higher than 20–30 min of light swimming or 5.2 km/h walking on separate days.

### Cited Findings
- **Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Med 52(3):601–612 (43 studies).** "Concurrent aerobic and strength training does not compromise muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength development." Explosive strength was attenuated, more so when both were done in the same session, and not when sessions were at least 3 h apart. Results did not depend on type of aerobic exercise, frequency, training status, or age. — [PubMed 34757594](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)
- Pooled SMDs from Schumann 2022, as reported by a secondary source: hypertrophy −0.01 (15 studies), maximal strength −0.06, explosive strength −0.28. — [FitCraft summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/concurrent-training-interference) (secondary; the figures match the paper's conclusion)
- **Lundberg et al. 2022, Sports Med 52:2391–2403 (15 studies, fibre level).** Overall fibre hypertrophy SMD −0.23 [−0.46 to −0.00]. Type I fibres −0.34, type II −0.13 (not significant). Type I fibres were affected with running (−0.81) but not with cycling. — [PubMed 35476184](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35476184/)

### Inferences
- The user's off-day aerobic work is low-intensity, short, separated from lifting by about 24 h, and non-running (swimming or walking). Every known moderator therefore points to negligible interference. This part of the plan is sound and may help recovery and work capacity.
- Swimming loads the shoulders and lats. After Mon Upper A (6 sets of pulldowns, 4 sets of incline press), a hard swim on Tue could add upper-body fatigue, so it should stay genuinely easy. Treadmill walking is the zero-risk alternative when the shoulders feel beaten up.
- The energy cost of 20–30 min of swimming or walking 2×/wk adds to TDEE. This matters for the surplus (see Q4).

### Gaps
- Wilson et al. 2012 (J Strength Cond Res; concurrent training meta-analysis that reported running interfering more than cycling, and dose dependence on frequency and duration) was not retrieved in this session. Its specific figures are unverified here.
- No trials specifically tested swimming as the concurrent modality for hypertrophy outcomes.

## Q4. Energy surplus for lean gain: is maintenance + 250 kcal appropriate?

### Takeaway
+250 kcal is roughly a 9–11% surplus at plausible maintenance levels. That sits inside the evidence-based range (Iraki 2019: about 10–20% for novice and intermediate lifters; Helms 2023: 5–20% scaled to experience). The bigger problem is that a Mifflin-St Jeor × activity estimate can be off by more than 250 kcal. Bodyweight trend should therefore drive the target: about 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week, which is about 0.18–0.37 kg/wk for 73 kg.

### Cited Findings
- **Iraki et al. 2019, Sports (Basel) 7(7):154 (narrative review).** For novice and intermediate lifters, "a hyper-energetic diet (~10–20%) should be consumed with a target weight gain of ~0.25–0.5% of bodyweight/week". For advanced lifters, about 0.25%/wk. Energy intake should be adjusted based on changes in body composition. — [PubMed 31247944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/); [PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/)
- **Helms et al. 2023, Sports Med – Open 9:102 (RCT, 21 trained lifters, 8 weeks, RT 3 d/wk).** Groups were maintenance, a 5% surplus, and a 15% surplus. Muscle thickness changes (VL, VI, RF, triceps) were similar across groups. The 15% group gained more skinfold thickness than maintenance (BF10 = 4.2). The 15% group improved bench 1-RM more than the others, while squat 1-RM was similar. Biceps showed weak evidence of benefiting from more weight gain. Authors' conclusion: faster weight gain "primarily increase[s] rates of fat gain rather than augmenting 1-RM or MT". They recommend "conservative energy surpluses … of 5–20% over maintenance … or rates of weight gain of 0.25–0.5% of body mass per week," with smaller surpluses for more advanced lifters. — [PMC10620361](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10620361/)
- **Slater et al. 2019, Front Nutr (review).** "Skeletal muscle hypertrophy is possible in an energy deficit," more likely in people who are untrained, overweight, or detrained. There is "clear evidence of an anabolic stimulus possible from an energy surplus." — [PMC6710320](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710320/); [PubMed 31482093](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31482093/)
- **Frankenfield et al. 2005, J Am Diet Assoc (systematic review).** Mifflin-St Jeor was "the most reliable, predicting RMR within 10% of measured" in more individuals than the other equations, but "noteworthy errors and limitations exist when it is applied to individuals." — [PubMed 15883556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15883556/); [ANDEAL PDF](https://www.andeal.org/files/Docs/Frankenfield_et_al_2005%5B1%5D.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Worked example.** At 73 kg, 0.25–0.5%/wk is about 0.18–0.37 kg/wk, or about 0.8–1.6 kg/month. Mifflin-St Jeor's ±10% RMR error alone is about ±170 kcal for a ~1,700 kcal RMR. An activity factor adds further error. That uncertainty is about the same size as the entire +250 kcal surplus, so the user could be at maintenance or at a +500 surplus without knowing it. Recommendation: weigh daily or several times a week, compare 7-day averages, and adjust intake by about 100–150 kcal if the 2–3 week trend falls outside 0.18–0.37 kg/wk.
- The activity factor should not double-count. The app should not apply a "very active" factor and also add the swim and walk calories on top.
- Fat at 25% of kcal fits typical sports-nutrition ranges (Iraki 2019 discusses fat as a proportion of energy). Carbs make up the rest, which supports training volume. These are sensible defaults.
- Given the Helms 2023 data, a recreational or intermediate lifter gets little extra muscle from going above about 10–15%. Staying at +250 is reasonable.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 RCT beyond Helms 2023 comparing surplus sizes was retrieved. The task brief's mention of "2024 trials" could not be confirmed.
- The user's actual maintenance estimate and activity factor are not in the program file, so the surplus percentage is approximate.

## Q5. Protein: is 2.0 g/kg reasonable? Distribution and pre-sleep protein

### Takeaway
Yes. 2.0 g/kg (about 146 g/day at 73 kg) sits between the Morton 2018 breakpoint of 1.62 g/kg and the upper 95% CI of 2.20 g/kg, which the authors suggest as a "prudent" target for maximising gains. Spreading it over about 4–5 meals of about 0.4 g/kg each (about 29 g) is the evidence-based way to distribute it.

### Cited Findings
- **Morton et al. 2018, Br J Sports Med 52(6):376–384 (49 RCTs, 1,863 participants).** Protein supplementation increased FFM gains by 0.30 kg [0.09–0.52]. The breakpoint was 1.62 g/kg/day [95% CI 1.03–2.20]. "It may be prudent to recommend ~2.2 g protein/kg/d for those seeking to maximise resistance training-induced gains in FFM." The effect was larger in resistance-trained individuals (0.75 kg [0.09–1.40]). — [PMC5867436](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/); [PubMed 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)
- **Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 15:10.** "To maximize anabolism one should consume protein at a target intake of 0.4 g/kg/meal across a minimum of four meals in order to reach a minimum of 1.6 g/kg/day." Using up to 0.55 g/kg/meal across 4 meals reaches 2.2 g/kg/day. — [PubMed 29497353](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/); [CUNY PDF](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1206&context=le_pubs)

### Inferences
- **For 73 kg.** 0.4 g/kg is about 29 g per meal, and 4 meals of that give about 117 g (1.6 g/kg). Reaching the app's 146 g takes either 4 meals of about 36–37 g (0.5 g/kg) or 5 meals of about 29 g. Either fits the Schoenfeld & Aragon framework.
- A protein-containing meal before bed (one of the 4–5 feedings) is a reasonable way to add the 4th or 5th feeding. The specific evidence on pre-sleep protein was not retrieved in this session.
- Keep 2.0 g/kg on rest days too. Muscle protein synthesis stays elevated for about 24–48 h after a session, so Tue, Thu and Sun are still anabolic days.

### Gaps
- No pre-sleep protein RCTs (e.g., Snijders 2015; Res 2012) were retrieved. Their specifics are unverified here.

## Q6. Supplements: creatine, magnesium + zinc (ZMA), caffeine

### Takeaway
Creatine monohydrate at 5 g/day is the best-supported supplement for adding to RT-induced hypertrophy, though the extra effect on directly measured muscle is small. Magnesium + zinc (ZMA) did nothing for training adaptations in resistance-trained men with adequate zinc status. Its only plausible value is correcting a deficiency. Caffeine was not researched in depth.

### Cited Findings
- **Kreider et al. 2017, ISSN position stand, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 14:18.** Creatine monohydrate is the most effective ergogenic supplement for high-intensity exercise capacity and lean mass during training, and it is safe at recommended doses. A typical maintenance dose is 3–5 g/day. These dosing details are standard ISSN content, but I did not quote them from page text this session; only the title and scope were confirmed. — [PMC5469049](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/); [PubMed 28615996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/)
- **Burke et al. 2023, Nutrients 15(9):2116 (systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs of at least 6 weeks with MRI, CT or ultrasound).** "Creatine supplementation combined with RT promotes a small increase in the direct measures of skeletal muscle hypertrophy in both the upper and lower body." The ResearchGate excerpt suggests a larger effect in younger participants. — [PubMed 37432300](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/15/9/2116)
- **Wilborn et al. 2004, J Int Soc Sports Nutr 1(2):12–20 (RCT, resistance-trained men, 8 weeks).** ZMA had no significant effect on total testosterone (p=0.50), free testosterone (p=0.96), IGF-1, GH, or cortisol. "ZMA supplementation during training does not appear to enhance training adaptations in resistance trained populations." — [PMC2129161](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2129161/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5349154_Effects_of_Zinc_Magnesium_Aspartate_ZMA_Supplementation_on_Training_Adaptations_and_Markers_of_Anabolism_and_Catabolism)

### Inferences
- Keep creatine at 5 g/day, at any time of day, taken daily including rest days. Timing is minor next to consistency. A rise of about 1–2 kg in scale weight in the first weeks is probably water and should not be read as fat gain when judging the surplus. That figure is general knowledge, not sourced this session.
- Magnesium + zinc in the evening is harmless at label doses for most people, but the app should not present it as an anabolic or testosterone aid. Evening timing is fine for sleep-routine reasons. Zinc and magnesium can compete for absorption with each other and with calcium or iron at high doses (general nutrition knowledge, unsourced here).
- **Caffeine (not researched this session).** The ISSN caffeine position stand (Guest et al. 2021) is generally cited for 3–6 mg/kg about 60 min before exercise. That would be about 220–440 mg for 73 kg, an unverified figure here. Main interaction: avoid it late in the day before evening sessions because it can impair sleep (see Q7).

### Gaps
- The full text of the ISSN 2017 creatine position stand was not quoted, and dosing specifics were not quoted verbatim.
- No caffeine-specific source was retrieved (Guest 2021 ISSN position stand not fetched).
- No study of magnesium or zinc for sleep quality in lifters was retrieved.

## Q7. Sleep and hypertrophy

### Takeaway
Even one night of total sleep deprivation cut postprandial muscle protein synthesis by 18% in a controlled trial (Lamon 2021). Sleep is therefore a real lever for gains, and the user's sleep/energy/drive tracking is useful. Long-term trials linking chronic short sleep to hypertrophy outcomes were not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **Lamon et al. 2021, Physiol Rep 9(1):e14660.** One night of total sleep deprivation in healthy young adults reduced postprandial muscle protein fractional synthesis rate by 18% compared with normal sleep, and shifted the hormonal environment in a catabolic direction. — [PubMed 33400856](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33400856/); [Wiley](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.14814/phy2.14660)

### Inferences
- **Using the tracked metrics.** If sleep (or energy or drive, 1–10) stays low for several days in a row, that is a reasonable trigger to (a) hold load progression and (b) drop the optional cold exposure, which adds sympathetic stress. Pre-set deload rules are an evidence-informed heuristic, not something a trial tested.
- Morning or daytime rest-day cold and sauna are less likely to disrupt sleep than late-evening sessions. This is a plausible inference; no study was retrieved.
- Late-evening caffeine works against the sleep lever.

### Gaps
- Dattilo et al. 2011 (Med Hypotheses; the hypothesis that sleep debt is catabolic via cortisol, testosterone and IGF-1) was not retrieved. It is a hypothesis paper, not an outcome trial.
- The exact hormonal figures in Lamon 2021 (cortisol and testosterone changes) were not confirmed from the page text this session. Only the 18% MPS reduction was confirmed.
- No RCT on chronic partial sleep restriction and long-term hypertrophy was retrieved.
